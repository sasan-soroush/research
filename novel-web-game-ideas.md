# Novel Web Game Ideas: Unique Angles Only

*October 2026. A follow-up to `big-web-game-ideas.md`, whose ideas were proven genres moved to the web ("Minecraft in a browser"). Every idea here has a **hook nobody owns yet**, a **reason it lasts for years**, and something **only the web can do**.*

---

## How I generated these

"X, but in a browser" is not an angle. I started from things **only the web has**, and asked what game each one makes possible:

| Web-only superpower | What it makes possible |
|---|---|
| **Everything is a link.** Every page, place or player can be a URL. | The internet itself can be the game map. |
| **Live real-world data**: news, Wikipedia page views, weather, time zones. | Live ops that run themselves, driven by the real world. |
| **No install, so a whole audience can join in 5 seconds.** | Thousands of stream viewers can become players mid-broadcast. |
| **The browser is always open**: new tabs, every site you visit. | A game that lives *alongside* your day rather than in a separate app. |
| **AI at web scale** | One opponent, or one historian, shared by millions of players. |
| **Real identity and location**: city, country, time zone. | Hometown pride as the team structure. |

Each idea below takes one of these to its logical extreme.

---

## 1. HYPERTERRA: the internet is the map ⭐ *My top pick*

**Hook:** A territory war played over the real link structure of Wikipedia. Every article is a territory, and every hyperlink is a road between two territories. Your clan starts on "Volcano" and pushes through "Pompeii" toward "Roman Empire", a capital-sized region because thousands of articles link to it.

**How it plays**
- **The map** is roughly 7 million real articles in English alone. Big hub articles (Countries, Science, Music) are valuable capitals. Obscure articles are cheap frontier land.
- **Resources** come from each article's real **page views**, which Wikimedia publishes openly. An article people are reading right now produces more resources.
- **Combat** is strategic (troops move along links), with optional skill duels: a quick trivia or "link race" fight drawn from the contested article's own text.
- **Live ops run themselves.** When something becomes world news, its article's page views spike and it becomes a gold rush overnight. Think of a World Cup final, an election or a celebrity album. **Every real-world headline is a free in-game event**, and you never run out.

**Why it's unique:** Link-race games like "The Wiki Game" exist, but those are single-player speed puzzles. **Nobody has built a persistent strategy MMO on the internet's knowledge graph, with real-world attention as its economy.**

**Why it lasts:** The map grows by itself as Wikipedia grows. The news never stops. Clans develop real identities: "We hold all of Jazz." It's also one of the few deep games teachers and parents could *like*, because players learn by accident.

**Built-in virality:** Every territory is a shareable link ("my clan owns the Taylor Swift article, come take it"). Streamers can fight over their own articles, and fandoms will defend their favourite topics.

**Money:** Clan subscriptions, cosmetic banners and flags shown on owned territories, a season pass, and sponsored "real event" seasons (an Olympics season).

**Cheap first test (6–8 weeks):** Use only the ~10,000 most-linked articles, one-week seasons, and clans of five. Launch on Discord, since clans map naturally onto servers. Measure whether clans come back daily to defend territory.

**Risks:**
- Wikipedia's *content* is open-licensed (CC BY-SA) and its page-view data is public. Its *name and logo* are trademarks, so never imply endorsement and credit it properly.
- Make sure the game never pushes people to edit Wikipedia to gain an advantage. Base mechanics on page views and links, never on edits.

## 2. HEIRLOOM: a world where history is permanent and AI writes it down

**Hook:** One shared, persistent fantasy world. Every player lives **one life per real-world week**. When your character dies, your heir inherits what you leave behind: land, grudges, debts and reputation. An **AI chronicler** turns everything players do into the world's **official history book**, a living wiki of wars, betrayals, dynasties and legends that anyone can read online.

**How it plays:** A small kingdom sim: farming, trade, politics, marriages between player families, and wars. Big decisions are irreversible. If you burn a city, it stays ash in the history, and in the map, forever.

**Why it's unique:**
- *One Hour One Life* proved generational play is gripping, but its worlds and stories vanish.
- **Heirloom's product is the accumulated history itself.** Year 5 of the world is impossible to copy, because no competitor can fake five years of real player history.
- The chronicle doubles as marketing: "The Betrayal at Greywater" can go viral like a true story, because it *is* a true story.

**Why it lasts:**
- Dynasties create multi-year commitment, because you're playing for your great-grandchildren.
- Lore piles up with no writing team.
- New players arrive into a world with real legends to live up to.

**Money:**
- A dynasty subscription: more heirs, family crest, a family archive.
- Cosmetic heraldry.
- "Commission a chronicle": an illustrated book of your family's history, sold as a print or PDF. Real merchandise that only your game can produce.

**Cheap first test:** A text-and-map game with 500 players for four real weeks, with AI chronicle entries every day. Test whether people read and share the history, and whether deaths create grief and loyalty rather than people quitting.

**Risks:**
- Permanence can feel punishing, so soften it with your dynasty's legacy bonuses.
- AI writing costs money, but entries can be written in daily batches rather than live.

## 3. THE ADVERSARY: all of humanity vs. one learning AI

**Hook:** Every player on Earth is on the same team, against **a single AI opponent that studies everyone at once** and adapts every week. It's a global co-op strategy game in which the whole player base is one side of the war.

**How it plays:**
- Each player defends a small sector in short sessions of tower defense, tactics or puzzle battles.
- The Adversary sees what *strategies* the community uses everywhere and counters them next week.
- Leaked "intelligence reports" explain what the AI has learned, so the community has to invent new tactics.
- Each season ends in a global **siege weekend**, streamed live, where humanity wins or loses together.

**Why it's unique:** Raid bosses are scripted, and the community solves them in days. **An enemy that keeps learning from the entire player base can never be fully solved.** The "meta" becomes a real arms race between millions of humans and one machine. That's also a story the press and YouTube love.

**Why it lasts:**
- The opponent is always new, so there's no content treadmill.
- A shared global identity ("humanity is losing, log in") pulls lapsed players back.
- Season outcomes become real events.

**Money:** Cosmetic squads and banners, a season pass, and "war bonds": a cosmetic investment tied to the global outcome, rewarding all buyers if humanity wins. Never sell power.

**Cheap first test:** A web tower-defense game with a weekly AI that changes its attacks based on the most common player defenses. The AI can start simple (statistics plus an LLM "director" choosing counters) before any heavy machine learning. Test whether "the AI adapted to us" talk spreads in the community.

**Risks:** The AI must feel clever but fair, so it needs to be tuned. Players are often more frustrated by an enemy that seems unbeatable than by one that is hard.

## 4. HOMETURF: your real hometown is your team

**Hook:** A permanent world map, like r/place but with strategy, where **you can only fight for the place you actually live**. Cities fight cities, and countries rise and fall. Day and night follow the real sun, so **time zones hand the war off around the world**: Asia attacks while Europe sleeps, then the Americas counterattack.

**How it plays:** You join your real city's army (verified by rough location, never your exact address). Short daily actions, such as building, defending and raiding neighbouring cities, add up to city-wide power. City councils are elected by players, and local leaders become minor celebrities.

**Why it's unique:**
- r/place drew millions with zero depth, and Wordle-style "country leaderboards" spread instantly.
- **Nobody has built a lasting game around local identity**, which is one of the strongest loyalties people have.
- The global time-zone relay is a cooperation mechanic no other game has.

**Why it lasts:**
- People don't stop living in their city.
- Rivalries are real: Manchester vs. Liverpool, Toronto vs. Montreal.
- Local media will cover it ("Our city just conquered Ohio").

**Money:**
- **Local sponsorship** is a new B2B model: real cafés, gyms and sports teams sponsor city buildings and banners.
- Cosmetic city pride gear.
- Season pass.

**Cheap first test:** Launch in two rival cities only, with a cheap local push (a subreddit and a local streamer). Measure whether city pride beats ordinary game motivation on day-7 retention.

**Risks:**
- Location privacy, so only use city-level data.
- Small towns need a fair chance, so let them join together into regions.
- Moderate nationalist toxicity carefully.

## 5. CROWDMASTER: the stream audience is the dungeon

**Hook:** One streamer plays a roguelike hero. **Thousands of viewers click a link and *become* the dungeon.** They place traps, spawn monsters and vote on boss fights, live and in their own browser, with no install.

**How it plays:**
- Viewers earn "dread" by watching and participating, then spend it on traps and monsters.
- The viewers who kill the streamer earn a spot on the stream's hall of fame.
- Viewers keep a **persistent dungeon-builder profile across every streamer** they watch, so the audience has its own progression.

**Why it's unique:**
- Twitch Plays Pokémon and Crowd Control proved viewers will pay to mess with streamers, but those are add-ons bolted onto other people's games.
- **Crowdmaster is a game *designed* for two sides: one performer and an audience of thousands.**
- The web is what makes it work, because viewers join in a single click mid-stream.

**Why it lasts:**
- Every streamer becomes your distribution channel and your live ops.
- Viewers build long-term identity and collections across streams.
- Smaller streamers get a growth tool, so they adopt it.

**Money:**
- Viewers pay for premium monsters and boss summons, and the **streamer gets a revenue share**. That aligns creators with you, the same flywheel that made Roblox's creator economy work.
- Cosmetic hero skins for streamers.

**Cheap first test:** Recruit 20 mid-size streamers for a two-week beta. Measure what percentage of viewers click in, and spend per 1,000 viewers.

**Risks:**
- You depend on Twitch, YouTube and Kick policies.
- Harassment through the game must be impossible, because viewers act only through game mechanics, never free text on screen.

## 6. FAMILIAR: a creature that lives on the web with you

**Hook:** A companion creature that lives in your new tab and walks across the websites you visit, using a browser extension plus a mobile app. When you open a page that **other players are reading at the same time, your creatures meet**. They play, trade and battle in the corner of a news article or recipe page. **The whole internet becomes a social space.**

**How it plays:**
- Your Familiar grows over months and years, shaped by your habits (opt-in): night owl, reader, gym-goer.
- Rare creatures appear on certain kinds of sites: a "library sprite" on long reads, a "tide spirit" on surf reports.
- Collecting is a natural loop: you discover where creatures live by browsing.

**Why it's unique:**
- Neopets and Webkinz had pets, and Pokémon Go turned the physical world into the map.
- **Familiar does that for the internet**: the web you already browse every day becomes the place you play, with zero dedicated play time.

**Why it lasts:**
- A pet you've raised for three years is emotionally impossible to delete.
- The game is present every day without asking for time, which builds a habit no other game can.

**Money:** Cosmetics, a care subscription, rare-creature passes, and limited-time items (with publisher permission, sites could host official creature events, which is a B2B channel).

**Cheap first test:** A Chrome extension with a new-tab pet and "presence" on the top 1,000 sites. Test 30-day retention and whether people screenshot their pets meeting.

**Risks:**
- Extension privacy must be strict and transparent. Process browsing data on the device and only send coarse site categories.
- Browser stores review extensions closely.

## 7. Wild card: HUMAN SIGNAL, a game funded by AI labs, not whales

**Hook:** A genuinely fun competitive puzzle and judgment game: debates, spot-the-flaw, taste duels, and "which answer is better" battles. The skill being trained is human judgment, and **AI companies pay for that data**. **Players earn real money or rewards; the business sells skilled human judgment to companies.**

**Why it's unique:**
- Foldit and reCAPTCHA showed games can do real work.
- In 2026, demand for high-quality human feedback data is enormous.
- No one has made a game where *being good at the game* is a valuable skill that companies pay for.

**Why it lasts:** Income gives players a reason to keep playing that cosmetics can't. Ranked "judge" tiers become a respected identity.

**Money:** B2B data contracts, with players paid a share. Revenue doesn't depend on player spending at all.

**Risks:**
- Fun vs. work balance.
- Data quality and anti-fraud.
- Labour and payment regulation in each country.
- It's a business-development-heavy company, not a pure game studio.

## Comparison

| Idea | Unique angle | Longevity engine | Revenue ceiling | Build difficulty | Novelty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hyperterra** | The internet's knowledge graph is the map; the news is the live ops | Endless map plus endless real events | High | Medium | ★★★★★ |
| **2. Heirloom** | Permanent player-made history, written by AI | History that piles up and can't be copied | High | Medium | ★★★★★ |
| **3. The Adversary** | Everyone vs. one learning AI | An opponent that never stops changing | High | High | ★★★★★ |
| **4. Hometurf** | Real hometown pride and a time-zone relay | Real-world identity and rivalries | Medium–High | Medium | ★★★★ |
| **5. Crowdmaster** | The audience is the dungeon | Streamers act as distribution | High | Medium–High | ★★★★ |
| **6. Familiar** | The whole web becomes the playground | A bond that grows over years, plus daily presence | High | Medium (extension risk) | ★★★★ |
| **7. Human Signal** | Players paid, AI labs pay | Income for players | Very high (B2B) | High (non-game work) | ★★★★★ |

## My pick

**Hyperterra**, ideally with Heirloom's AI chronicle layered on top, so every season produces a readable "history of the war for the internet".

- **Unique, and easy to say in one sentence:** "A strategy MMO where Wikipedia is the map and the news drives the events."
- **Content that costs almost nothing:** the map and the live events are generated by the real world, which solves the biggest cost of any live-service game.
- **Viral by design:** every territory is a link, every headline is an event, and every fandom has a reason to show up.
- **Cheap to test:** a 10,000-article prototype in 6–8 weeks tells you whether clans fight for territory.
- **Hard to copy once it's running:** clan histories and territory ownership pile up, just like Heirloom's history.
